Lesson 16 · Leadership Principles · Work Style and behavioural prep

Know the principles well enough to answer as yourself

About 30 minutes · Your win: you can name the principle behind a work statement, settle the classic tensions the way Amazon does, and have two interview stories drafted

Every part of Amazon's hiring is tied to the 16 Leadership Principles. The Work Style assessment checks how you approach work against them, and in interviews each interviewer takes one principle and asks about your past. You can't prepare by memorising "right answers", and Amazon designs against that. What you can do is understand each principle so well that you recognise it in any statement, and know where you genuinely stand.

What the Work Style assessment is

  • Amazon describes it as rating how well statements represent your work style, built around its culture and the Leadership Principles, taking about 10 to 20 minutes (Amazon: assessments, as quoted in search results. I didn't read the page directly).
  • Third-party prep sites disagree on the exact format. Some say pairs of statements where you pick one, others say an agree-to-disagree scale. Treat any claimed format as unconfirmed.
  • How to answer: honestly and consistently. The same trait gets asked several ways, so answers chosen to look good tend to contradict each other. Read each statement, ask yourself "which principle is this about?", then answer for the person you actually are at work or school.

The 16 principles

Read the Leadership Principles card first, for about 5 minutes. It has Amazon's definition and Jassy's main point for each. Then close it and try the questions below from memory.

Part 1: which principle is this?

These are practice statements written for this lesson. They aren't real assessment items.

"When I notice a broken process outside my team, I find out who owns it. If nobody does, I fix it myself."

"Our dashboard says 99.5% of orders are fine, but a customer emailed about a problem, so I traced their order myself."

"If a decision is easy to undo, I'd rather make it today and adjust than wait two weeks for complete data."

"When I get something wrong, I say so in front of the team, even when it's embarrassing."

"I argued against the plan in the meeting. Once the team chose it, I put my full effort into making it work."

"Before I build something, I write down who it's for and what problem it solves for them."

Part 2: when two principles pull against each other

The hardest statements set two good things against each other. Jassy names the tensions himself and says how they're settled.

A teammate wants to wait two weeks for more data before a choice that could be reversed in a day. What fits Amazon's principles best?

You argued against a plan and lost. Two weeks later it's struggling, just as you predicted. What now?

You could ship a feature today, but you know one checkout case gives customers a wrong total. Shipping is reversible. What fits?

Part 3: start your story bank

In interviews, behavioural questions come as "Tell me about a time…", and Amazon expects real examples in STAR form: Situation, Task, Action, Result (see the card). Most of your time goes on Action, and you say "I", not "we". Stories can come from school, projects, jobs, clubs or this study plan itself. Draft two now. It saves as you type.
